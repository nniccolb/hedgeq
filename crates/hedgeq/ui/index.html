Budgeted Adaptive Request Hedging

hedgeqTechnical noteSource code

Abstract

In services that fan a request out to many replicated shards, occasional slow servers dominate user-visible latency. Sending a duplicate request to another replica (hedging) reduces this tail, at the cost of extra load. A hedge delay fixed in advance cannot follow changes in traffic. We learn the delay online and enforce a hard limit on extra requests. When traffic increases, this gives a markedly lower tail than a fixed delay under the same limit.

1Experiment 1: one page load

Consider an analytics page: a 3D heatmap of live events by location, with totals. The events are spread across 20 shards, each stored on 3 replicas. Every column height and every total is a sum over all shards, so nothing can be drawn until the slowest shard has answered.

Most shard queries are fast, about 30 ms. But a server occasionally stalls (here 3% of the time, at 40 times slower: garbage collection, a noisy neighbour, a compaction), so with 20 shards some shard is usually stuck behind a stall and the whole page waits for it. Hedging sends a backup copy of a slow query to another replica; we cap extra requests at 10% of traffic. Figure 1 replays page loads from a simulation of this service; both pages receive identical requests and identical work (seed 1).

2Experiment 2: fifty page loads at once

A busy service never serves a single page. Here fifty different pages are requested at the same instant, each assembled from the same 20 shards (fifty distinct recorded loads from the simulation). 4Limitations

Results are from a simulation with simple queueing and cooperative cancellation, not a production system. With a steady load the adaptive and fixed delays perform about equally; the advantage appears when conditions change. A fixed delay without a limit can reach lower latency, but only by sending several times its intended extra load. Details and further scenarios are in the repository.
