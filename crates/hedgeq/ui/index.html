Abstract

In services that fan a request out to many replicated shards, rare per-server stalls become the typical user experience: the response is as slow as its slowest part. Hedging, sending a duplicate to another replica after a delay, repairs the tail, but a fixed delay silently over- or under-spends as load and failure rates drift. We learn the delay online from a decaying, relative-error quantile sketch, steer it with a feedback controller toward a fraction of the budget, and enforce the budget absolutely with a token bucket. In a deterministic simulator with paired random numbers, the adaptive policy stayed under its budget in every scenario tested, matched a fixed delay in steady state, and cut p99.9 by roughly 1.4–2.3× when load or failure rates drifted, at the same cap.

Fan-out turns rare slowness into the common case

03 · Method

Three small mechanisms, one guarantee

Nothing exotic: a sketch, a controller and a bucket. Each is a few dozen lines of Rust and each is unit-tested.

01 · OBSERVE

Decaying quantile sketch

First-response latencies go into a log-bucketed histogram with ≤1% relative error. Weights decay with a 10-second half-life, so the estimate follows the current regime instead of averaging over history.

02 · CONTROL

Closed-loop delay

The hedge delay is the (1−τ) quantile of the sketch. Every 100 ms a multiplicative controller nudges the tail mass τ so that hedge demand settles at 70% of the budget, leaving headroom for bursts.

03 · CAP

Token-bucket budget

Each primary request earns ε tokens; each hedge spends one. Whatever the controller or the workload does, hedges sent never exceed ε × primaries + B. The bound is checked by a property test against an adversary that always asks.

hedges_sent  ≤  ε · primaries + B          // always, by construction
delay(t)     =  Qsketch(t)(1 − τ(t))        // τ steered toward 0.7ε

Why a fixed delay fails. A delay tuned at the 95th percentile of last week's latencies hedges far too eagerly when load or failure rates rise, and the extra load itself makes things slower. Try the Load surge scenario above: the uncapped fixed policy quietly spends several times its design budget, and the capped one, stuck with a stale delay, spends its tokens on the wrong requests and its tail balloons.

04 · Threats to validity

What this does and does not show

  • Results come from a simulator, not a production system. The model is deliberately simple: single-server FIFO queues, log-normal service times, on/off slowdown episodes, instant cooperative cancellation.
  • Slowdown is sampled when a request starts service; an episode that begins mid-request does not slow that request.
  • Hedging helps only when slowness is not shared by all replicas. With fully correlated stragglers, no hedging policy can help (see the Correlated stragglers scenario).
  • The uncapped fixed policy can beat every capped policy on latency in a regime shift. It does so by exceeding its budget several times over, which is exactly the risk the cap exists to remove.
  • Primary replicas are chosen uniformly at random; real load balancers do better, which would shrink absolute tails for every policy.
